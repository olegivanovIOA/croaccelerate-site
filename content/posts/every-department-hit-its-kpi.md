Every month the reports look fine. Procurement shows savings. Marketing delivered more leads than planned. Production hit its output target. Each head of department can defend their number.

And the company still misses its quarter.

I have seen this pattern in IT services, in marketing companies and now in manufacturing. It is rarely a people problem. Employees are usually doing exactly what the system asks of them. The problem sits **between** the reports — at the seams where one function's responsibility ends and someone else starts paying for its result.

## Where the money actually leaks

A few seams show up again and again.

**Procurement → production.** Cheaper raw material lowers purchase cost by a few percent. Six weeks later the defect rate creeps up, and so does scrap, rework and machine time. Procurement reports a saving; production reports a quality problem; nobody connects the two because they live in different spreadsheets.

**Marketing → sales.** Marketing is measured on leads, so it buys leads. Sales is measured on closed deals, so it ignores the ones it cannot close. In one company I worked with, most declined leads were screened out before a salesperson ever saw them — and that screening pipeline had never been included in any report. Marketing looked successful, sales looked busy, and the cost per profitable customer was invisible.

**Finance → reality.** A financial model can be mathematically flawless and still describe a business that no longer exists. Plans built on last year's assumptions keep being "on track" right up until the cash says otherwise.

**Meetings → execution.** When facts arrive late and different teams bring different numbers, every meeting reopens the same question. Decisions are discussed, agreed, and discussed again. The owner ends up holding manual control, because they are the only person who sees across all the reports.

## Why departmental KPIs make it worse

KPIs are not the villain. The problem is that most of them are designed **per function** while results are paid for **by the whole company**.

A purchase-price KPI does not see defect cost. A lead-volume KPI does not see margin per customer. An output KPI does not see idle machine-hours on the sites that are waiting for orders. Each metric is correct; the combination tells the wrong story.

So the question I ask first is not "which department is failing?" but:

> Where does this team's responsibility end — and who pays for its result next?

## How to fix the seams

In practice, three moves do most of the work.

### 1. Register the metrics before you build dashboards

List every management metric with its owner, formula, source and how it is refreshed. You will immediately find metrics that no one owns, three versions of the same number, and decisions that rely on figures nobody can trace. In one group I helped build a registry of 168 metrics; the share that was fully automated became a KPI of its own and grew from 45% to 81% in about ten weeks.

### 2. Put connected functions on one screen

The seams only become visible when related numbers sit next to each other: purchase price next to defect cost, ad spend next to won deals and margin, output next to idle capacity. That is the real job of a [live management dashboard](/services/digital-control-tower/) — not prettier charts, but one shared view that ends the "my number versus your number" debate. You can see how this looked inside a [multi-site manufacturing group](/cases/manufacturing-group-control-tower/).

### 3. Measure the handover, not just the department

Add a few metrics that belong to no single team: margin per marketing campaign, cost of defects as a share of revenue, time from lead to first reply, time from decision to execution. These are the numbers that reveal whether the system works — and they are usually the ones missing.

## What this is not

This is not a call for more reporting. Most companies already produce too many reports. It is a call for **fewer, connected** numbers that everyone trusts — and for honesty about where the business really is, rather than a polished version of it.

It is also not something AI fixes on its own. AI agents are excellent at watching connected data and flagging when a seam starts to leak. They are useless if the data underneath disagrees with itself. Fix the seams first; automate second.

## Frequently asked questions

### Why do companies miss targets when every department hits its KPIs?

Because departmental KPIs measure each function in isolation, while costs and revenue move across functions. Savings or gains in one place are often paid for in another, and no single report shows the transfer.

### What are cross-functional KPIs?

Metrics that span a handover between teams — for example margin per marketing campaign, defect cost linked to purchased material, or time from lead to first reply. They expose the seams that departmental KPIs hide.

### Do we need new software to fix this?

Usually not at first. Most of the data already exists in your CRM, production records, ad platforms and finance sheets. The first step is a metric registry and automated pipelines into one shared view; software choices come later.

### Where should we start?

Pick one seam that clearly costs money — often marketing-to-sales or procurement-to-production — and put both sides on one screen.
